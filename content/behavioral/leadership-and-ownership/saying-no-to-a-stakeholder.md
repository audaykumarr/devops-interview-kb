## Question

"Tell me about a time you had to push back on or say no to a stakeholder's request — for example, a deadline or feature request you believed would compromise security or reliability." How should you approach answering this, and what is the interviewer actually assessing?

## Short Answer

The interviewer is checking whether you can hold a technical or safety line under real business pressure without either caving to keep the peace or being needlessly obstructive — a strong answer shows you understood the stakeholder's actual business need, explained the real risk in terms they could evaluate (not just technical jargon), and worked toward either a genuine alternative or an informed, explicit trade-off decision, rather than a flat, unexplained "no."

## Detailed Explanation

DevOps and platform roles regularly sit at the intersection of business pressure (ship faster, cut this corner just this once) and real technical/security/reliability risk, and how someone navigates that tension — without either being a pushover who lets risk through unchecked, or being reflexively obstructive in a way that damages trust and gets worked around — is a genuinely important, assessable skill.

**Did you understand the stakeholder's actual need before pushing back?** A strong answer shows you engaged with why the deadline or request mattered to them — a genuine business reason, not just "they wanted it" — since understanding the real need is often what lets you find a genuine alternative rather than a flat refusal.

**Did you explain the risk in terms the stakeholder could actually evaluate?** Saying no effectively to a non-technical or differently-technical stakeholder means translating the risk into terms they can weigh against their own priorities — "this could mean X hours of downtime affecting Y customers" lands very differently than "this violates best practice," and a strong answer shows this kind of translation happened, not just technical language repeated more insistently.

**Did you offer a path forward, not just a blocker?** The strongest version of this story usually isn't "I said no and that was the end of it" — it's "I said no to the original request, but here's the alternative we found that addressed their real need without the risk," or "we made an informed, deliberate trade-off together, with the risk explicitly acknowledged, rather than it being silently accepted or silently ignored."

**How did the actual disagreement get resolved, and was the outcome honest?** As with the technical-disagreement question, the most credible answers aren't uniformly "and I was right and they backed down" — sometimes the honest answer is "we found a middle path," or "leadership made the call to accept the risk anyway, with it explicitly documented, and that was a legitimate outcome even though I'd raised the concern."

**Did you maintain the relationship, not just win the argument?** A subtle but important signal: does the story show you preserved a working relationship with this stakeholder afterward, or does it sound like the interaction damaged trust — the former suggests you handled the disagreement in a way that's sustainable for ongoing collaboration, which matters more than winning any single specific argument.

## Key Takeaways

- The interviewer is assessing whether you can hold a real technical/safety line under business pressure without either caving or being needlessly obstructive.
- Understand the stakeholder's actual underlying need, and translate risk into terms they can genuinely evaluate, rather than repeating technical language more insistently.
- The strongest answers offer a genuine alternative or lead to an informed, explicit trade-off decision — not just a flat "no" with nothing constructive following it.
- Show the relationship was preserved afterward, not just that you won the specific disagreement — sustainable collaboration matters more than any single argument.

## Interview Follow-Up Questions

- How would you handle it if leadership overruled your concern and explicitly accepted the risk — what would you do next?
- How do you calibrate how hard to push back, given not every risk is worth significant friction with a stakeholder?
- Can you describe a time the reverse happened — a stakeholder pushed back on your technical recommendation, and they turned out to be right?
