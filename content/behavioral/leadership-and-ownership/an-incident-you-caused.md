## Question

"Tell me about a time you caused a production incident. What happened, and how did you handle it?" How should you approach answering this, and why does an interviewer specifically want a story where you were at fault, rather than one where you were the hero who fixed someone else's mistake?

## Short Answer

The interviewer is checking for genuine accountability and blameless-postmortem thinking applied to yourself specifically — do you own the mistake honestly without excessive self-flagellation or deflection, focus on the systemic fix rather than just personal contrition, and actually change something afterward. A candidate who claims to have never caused an incident either lacks real production experience or isn't being honest, and both are red flags — a strong answer picks a real mistake, explains it plainly, and shows what you and the team learned and changed as a result.

## Detailed Explanation

This question is deliberately asking for a story where you're the one who made the mistake, not the one who swooped in to fix someone else's — and that's specifically because how someone handles their own failure is a much sharper signal than how they handle someone else's. Anyone can look good while fixing another person's mistake; being honest and constructive about your own is harder and more revealing.

**Do you own it honestly, without minimizing or deflecting?** A strong answer plainly states what went wrong and that it was, genuinely, your action that caused it — not "we made a decision that in hindsight..." when it was actually your specific unilateral action, and not burying the actual mistake in a lot of context about mitigating circumstances before getting to the point.

**Do you also avoid excessive self-flagellation?** The opposite failure mode is also worth avoiding — spending the whole answer being visibly hard on yourself doesn't actually demonstrate the thing being assessed, which is calm, functional ownership. The best answers are matter-of-fact: this happened, here's what I did about it, here's what changed.

**Did you focus on the systemic fix, not just personal contrition?** Echoing the blameless-postmortem principle applied to yourself: the strongest answers explain what changed afterward at a process or system level (a new safeguard, a changed deployment practice, a new check) that would catch this class of mistake in the future, not just "I'll be more careful next time" — the latter isn't a real fix, since it depends on the same fallible human vigilance that already failed once.

**Did you handle the actual incident response well, separate from having caused it?** A genuinely strong answer often shows you responding to your own mistake with the same clear-headed incident-response discipline you'd want from anyone — communicating status, working the actual fix, not panicking or hiding what happened — which is itself a meaningful part of the signal, separate from the mistake itself.

**Being honest that it happened at all, and not picking a trivially minor "mistake."** Picking an example so small it barely counts as a real incident (a typo that was caught in review) undermines the answer's credibility — interviewers can generally tell when a candidate is dodging the actual question by choosing an artificially safe example.

## Key Takeaways

- The interviewer wants a genuine mistake specifically because how you handle your own failure is a sharper signal than how you handle someone else's.
- Own the mistake plainly and specifically, without deflecting to "we" when it was your unilateral action, and without excessive self-flagellation that doesn't actually demonstrate functional ownership.
- Focus on the systemic fix that changed afterward (a new safeguard, a changed practice), not just personal resolve to "be more careful" — the latter isn't a durable fix.
- Choose a genuinely real, non-trivial example — a safely minor "mistake" undermines credibility and reads as dodging the actual question.

## Interview Follow-Up Questions

- How did you communicate about the incident to your team or stakeholders afterward?
- What would you do differently if a similar situation arose again, given the systemic fix you described?
- How do you distinguish between an honest mistake and a case of genuine negligence, in yourself or others?
