## Question

"Tell me about a time you had multiple urgent, competing priorities from different stakeholders at once. How did you decide what to work on first, and how did you communicate that decision?" How should you approach answering this, and what does the interviewer actually want to see?

## Short Answer

The interviewer is checking whether you have a genuine, reasoned approach to prioritization under real conflicting pressure, rather than either defaulting to whoever's loudest or freezing under the competing demands — a strong answer describes the actual criteria you used to prioritize (real business/technical impact, not just seniority of who's asking), how you communicated the trade-off transparently to the deprioritized stakeholders rather than just silently deciding, and an honest account of how the situation actually played out.

## Detailed Explanation

DevOps and platform roles routinely involve juggling requests from multiple teams simultaneously — an urgent production issue, a deadline-driven feature request from one team, a security finding needing remediation from another — and how someone actually navigates genuinely competing, legitimate priorities (not a contrived scenario, but real conflicting demands) is a meaningful, assessable skill.

**What criteria did you actually use to prioritize?** A strong answer describes concrete reasoning — actual business or technical impact, risk if something isn't addressed, genuine urgency versus perceived urgency — rather than defaulting to whoever escalated loudest or whoever has the most organizational seniority, which is a common but weak default that optimizes for politics rather than actual impact.

**Did you communicate the trade-off transparently, rather than silently deciding?** A key part of handling this well is telling the deprioritized stakeholders directly what you're doing and why, rather than either avoiding the conversation or letting them find out passively that their request wasn't addressed — proactive communication about a deprioritization decision preserves trust even when someone doesn't get what they wanted immediately.

**Did you distinguish genuine urgency from perceived urgency?** Not everything labeled "urgent" by the person asking actually is, and a strong answer often shows judgment in this distinction — asking clarifying questions to understand real impact and timeline, rather than taking every stated urgency at face value, which is itself a mature prioritization skill worth demonstrating.

**Did you escalate for help when genuinely overloaded, rather than silently struggling?** Part of good prioritization judgment is recognizing when the actual volume or conflict of competing priorities exceeds what one person can reasonably resolve alone, and asking for help (from a manager, or surfacing the conflict to whoever can make a resourcing or priority call) rather than trying to silently absorb an unreasonable load — this is often a stronger signal than showing you personally muscled through everything.

**Honest about the actual trade-offs made.** A credible answer usually involves some real cost — something genuinely didn't get done as fast as its stakeholder wanted, and that's fine to say plainly, since the point of the question is your prioritization reasoning and communication, not that you somehow satisfied everyone simultaneously despite genuinely competing demands.

## Key Takeaways

- The interviewer wants to see genuine prioritization reasoning based on actual impact, not defaulting to whoever's loudest or most senior.
- Communicate deprioritization decisions transparently to affected stakeholders rather than silently deciding and letting them discover it passively.
- Distinguish genuine urgency from perceived urgency by asking clarifying questions, rather than taking every "urgent" label at face value.
- Recognizing when to escalate for help under genuinely unreasonable competing load is often a stronger signal than showing you personally absorbed everything.

## Interview Follow-Up Questions

- How did the stakeholder whose request was deprioritized react, and how did you manage that relationship afterward?
- How would you handle a situation where two stakeholders each insisted their request was the highest priority, with no clear objective way to resolve it?
- What systems or processes would you put in place to make this kind of prioritization decision more structured going forward?
