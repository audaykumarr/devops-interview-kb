## Question

"How would you handle disagreeing with a technical decision differently if it came from someone significantly more senior than you, versus a peer?" What's the interviewer actually probing for here, and how should the answer differ from a peer-disagreement story?

## Short Answer

The interviewer is checking whether seniority changes *how carefully and respectfully* you raise a concern without changing *whether* you raise it at all — a good answer shows more deliberate preparation (better evidence, choosing the right moment and setting) when pushing back on someone senior, not silence out of deference, and not identical bluntness to how you'd push back on a peer either. The core signal being tested: can you disagree upward without either caving silently or being needlessly confrontational.

## Detailed Explanation

This question exists because seniority genuinely does change the dynamics of disagreement, and pretending otherwise (either "I'd treat it exactly the same" or "I'd defer to their experience") both read as underdeveloped answers. The interviewer wants evidence of situational judgment, not a fixed script applied regardless of context.

What changes with a senior person: the stakes of being wrong about your own concern are often perceived as higher (junior pushback that turns out to be off-base can cost more credibility than peer pushback would), so it's worth investing more in preparation — having concrete evidence or a specific scenario ready, rather than a vague unease, and choosing a more deliberate setting (a 1:1 conversation rather than raising it in a group meeting where a senior person might feel more need to defend their position publicly). It's also worth being more explicit about *why* you're raising it — framing it as "I want to understand this better" or "I want to make sure I'm not missing something" can open a genuine dialogue more effectively than a flat assertion that might read (unintentionally) as more confrontational than intended, given the seniority gap.

What *shouldn't* change: whether you raise a real, well-founded concern at all. An answer that implies "I'd just defer because they're more senior" is actually a red flag for interviewers evaluating platform/DevOps roles specifically, because those roles often exist precisely to catch reliability/security risks that a senior person, moving fast on a feature, might not have fully considered — silent deference on a real technical risk is a liability, not a virtue, regardless of who proposed it.

A strong answer names this explicitly: more care in *how* the concern is raised (preparation, setting, framing) with someone senior, but no change in *whether* it gets raised — and ideally includes a concrete example showing exactly that distinction in practice, not just an assertion of the principle.

## Key Takeaways

- Seniority should change how carefully and respectfully a concern is raised — better preparation, a more deliberate setting — not whether it gets raised at all.
- An answer implying pure deference to seniority is a red flag, especially for reliability/security-adjacent roles where silent deference on real risk is a genuine liability.
- Framing pushback as a genuine question ("help me understand...") rather than a flat assertion often opens dialogue more effectively across a seniority gap.
- A concrete example demonstrating this distinction in practice is far more convincing than stating the principle abstractly.

## Interview Follow-Up Questions

- Can you give a specific example where you raised a concern with someone senior and it turned out you were wrong — how did you handle that?
- How would you handle it if, after raising the concern carefully, the senior person still disagreed and the decision went their way?
- What would you do differently if this were a public forum (a large team meeting) rather than a private conversation?
