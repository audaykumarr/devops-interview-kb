## Question

"Tell me about a time someone else's pushback changed your mind, rather than you convincing them." Why do interviewers deliberately ask for the "I was wrong" version of the disagreement story, and what makes an answer to this actually land well?

## Short Answer

This question specifically screens for intellectual honesty and updateability — it's easy to prepare a "I disagreed and I was right" story, and interviewers know it; asking for the reverse checks whether a candidate can genuinely recognize being wrong in the moment and change course, which is a stronger signal of good engineering judgment than always being right, since nobody actually always is. A strong answer names specifically *what* changed your mind (the actual argument or evidence, not just "they made a good point") and shows genuine attitude change, not a grudging technical concession while still believing you were right.

## Detailed Explanation

Interviewers ask this specifically because the more common "tell me about a disagreement" question invites a prepared, self-flattering narrative where the candidate was right all along — which is a weak signal on its own, since it's easy to cherry-pick a story where that happened to be true. Asking explicitly for the opposite — a time you were wrong and someone else changed your mind — is much harder to fake convincingly and reveals something more useful: can this person actually update their position based on new information, or do they just perform open-mindedness while quietly holding their ground.

What separates a strong answer from a weak one here is specificity and genuineness. A weak answer stays vague ("someone made a good point and I agreed") without naming the actual argument or evidence that shifted the thinking — which reads as either not remembering clearly or not having genuinely engaged with the counter-argument in the first place. A strong answer names the specific piece of evidence, data, or reasoning that did the actual work of changing the position — "I was convinced the extra abstraction layer was worth the complexity, until they showed me the actual latency numbers from a similar system, which made the trade-off obviously not worth it for our use case" is concrete and verifiable in a way "they had a good point" isn't.

The other signal worth demonstrating: genuine attitude change versus grudging technical concession. "I updated the code because they were technically right, but I still think my approach was better" is a subtly different (and weaker) answer than one that shows the candidate's actual understanding of the problem shifted, not just their willingness to comply with a technically-correct counter-argument while still believing their original instinct. The former reads as compliance under pressure; the latter reads as real learning — which is what the question is actually trying to surface.

## Key Takeaways

- This question specifically counters the easy-to-fake "I disagreed and was right" narrative by asking for the harder, more honest reverse case.
- A strong answer names the specific argument, evidence, or data that actually changed the position — vague "they made a good point" answers are a weak signal.
- Genuine attitude change (understanding actually shifted) reads much stronger than grudging technical compliance (did what was asked while still believing the original instinct was right).
- The ability to update on new information is a stronger predictor of good engineering judgment than a track record of always being right.

## Interview Follow-Up Questions

- How do you personally distinguish, in the moment, between "I should update my position" and "I should hold firm and explain my reasoning better"?
- Can you think of a time you initially resisted feedback and later realized you should have updated sooner — what made the difference?
- How would you create a team culture where people feel safe changing their mind publicly, without it being seen as a loss of credibility?
